_Epistemic status: I'm writing this before I turn 22, so I'm almost certainly wrong about some of it, possibly the important parts. I have also been, at various points in my life, a version of the man I'm criticizing. Discount for both. This essay pulls together arguments scattered across my earlier posts, on the romance market, on virtue signalling, and on pornography, into a single diagnosis that I hope is coherent._

One more thing before we start. This is not a men's rights manifesto, an incel blackpill, or a sermon about returning to some natural state of masculinity. If you came here looking for proof that women are to blame, you have the wrong address. The thing I'm blaming, explicitly and consistently, is men, or more precisely the patterns of male behaviour that, added up, damage almost every system they touch.

## I.

Something is happening to men, and I mean that literally: it shows up in data you can count, including in deaths.

In the United States, men die by suicide at almost four times the rate of women. The figure has been consistent for decades, and the gap isn't closing. Deaths of despair (suicide, overdose, alcohol-related illness) disproportionately strike working-age men, especially men without a university degree. Anne Case and Angus Deaton at Princeton documented this in painful detail.

On the education side, the gap now runs in a direction that surprises people. In nearly every OECD country, women outperform men at every level of education. In the US, women earn about 60% of bachelor's degrees and 63% of master's degrees, and in Indonesia women's participation in higher education has been ahead of men's for several years. Richard Reeves at the Brookings Institution, who is nobody's idea of a conservative (he ran strategy for Nick Clegg, the Liberal Democrat deputy prime minister in the UK), wrote an entire book about this, _Of Boys and Men_.

In the labour market, the share of prime-age men (25 to 54) who are neither working nor looking for work has been climbing since the 1960s. Economists call this labour-force non-participation, which is different from unemployment: unemployment assumes you're still looking, and these are men who have left the game entirely. In the US the figure is now around 11%, up from about 3% in the mid-20th century. Structural changes in the economy explain part of that rise, and I don't think they explain all of it.

The most striking numbers may be the ones on loneliness. A 2021 survey by the Survey Center on American Life found that the share of men who say they have no close friends rose from 3% in 1990 to 15% in 2021, a fivefold increase in three decades. Women saw an increase too, but nowhere near that size.

At the most intimate end of the data, the General Social Survey shows the share of young men (18 to 29) reporting no sexual activity in the past twelve months rising from about 10% in 2008 to roughly 27% in 2018. The same figure for young women stayed relatively stable.

I want to pause on something that may sound odd in an essay that is about to criticize men fairly hard: this data is sad. Behind each of these percentages are people who can't sleep, who have no one to call, and who can't find a reason to get up in the morning, and in the worst cases they decide there's no reason to get up at all.

If your first reaction is "well, that's the karma of patriarchy" or "finally, men feel what women feel", I understand the impulse, but I think it's empirically wrong and strategically dangerous. Empirically wrong, because the man dying by suicide in rural West Virginia is not the man sitting on a Fortune 500 board. "Men" is not a monolith, and handing out categorical consequences to a deeply heterogeneous group is exactly the habit of thought we should have outgrown. Strategically dangerous, because (and I'll argue this throughout) men who break rarely break alone. They take the people around them down too.

I should draw one distinction early. Saying "there is a problem with men" is very different from saying "men are victims", and I'm only saying the first. Men still hold most of the political, economic, and institutional power in almost every society on Earth. The asymmetry between men's and women's experience of sexual violence, harassment, and physical danger is real, and I don't intend to minimize it here or anywhere else.

What I am saying is more specific, and I think more useful. Something is mechanically broken in how a large share of young men live their lives, and the breakage has externalities that everyone else pays for, including (maybe especially) women.

So I'll skip the question of whether to feel sorry for men (a little, the way you'd feel sorry for anyone who has agency and refuses to use it) and ask a more useful one: what, precisely, is broken, and can it be fixed, or at least understood well enough that we stop arguing about symptoms and start diagnosing the disease?

My suspicion is that the answer is something neither side of the political spectrum likes to say. The left blames patriarchy; the right, and the manosphere, blame feminism. I think the main problem is older and more embarrassing than either of those: men are weak. I'll define that word carefully in a moment, because it's easy to misread, but the short version is that the weakness I mean is chosen. It's a long series of small choices that accumulate into character, or more precisely into the absence of it.

Added up across millions of men, that weakness pollutes almost every system that ought to work: the romance market, public discourse, community health, even politics. Weak men don't have to be evil for this to happen. In economic terms, a weak man is a negative externality that walks and breathes.

This is going to be uncomfortable to read; it was uncomfortable to write. If you're a man, there's a non-trivial chance you'll recognize yourself in some of what follows. I recognized myself. I'd take that as a reason to keep reading.

## II.

The word "weak" is loaded, I know. In gender discourse, calling men weak sounds like something an alpha-male podcast host says right before selling you a 499-dollar course on proper mewing, or a get-rich-in-30-days trading bootcamp. So I need to define what I mean, and just as importantly what I don't mean, before going any further.

Start with what I don't mean, since that's where most misreadings will come from.

First, poverty. A man who works two shifts to feed his family on the minimum wage is not a weak man. He might be the strongest man in any room he walks into. Strength is about what you do with the resources you have, whatever they happen to be.

Second, introversion. A man who hates parties and small talk and would rather read alone on a Friday night is not weak either. Social preference isn't a measure of character.

Third, failure. A man who starts a business and goes bankrupt, who confesses his feelings and gets rejected, who tries and falls short, isn't displaying weakness. If anything, that's a prerequisite for strength, since you can't become strong without ever bearing the consequences of an effort that didn't work.

Fourth, mental illness. Clinical depression, anxiety disorders, and PTSD are medical conditions that need medical care. Confusing illness with weakness of character is a stupidity that has already hurt men for long enough, and I'm not going to add to it.

Here's the operational definition I'll use for the rest of the essay: _a weak man is a man who refuses agency over his own life._ He has choices (a man without choices is oppressed, which is a different category), but choosing costs something he isn't willing to pay: discomfort, risk, the possibility of failure, and heaviest of all, responsibility for the outcome.

So this is a narrow kind of weakness, located in a person's relationship with agency: the capacity and willingness to make choices that carry consequences, and then to live with the consequences.

I see five patterns that show up fairly consistently. I'll describe them as symptoms of one underlying syndrome, which I think is more accurate, and more useful, than calling them sins.

### 1. Nihilism without construction

Some nihilism is productive. Nietzsche wrote about this (yes, I'm aware that quoting Nietzsche in an essay on masculinity is close to self-parody by now; bear with me). He never meant the death of God as an end point. The dismantling of old values was supposed to be a transition toward creating new ones, toward the Übermensch, the man who makes his own meaning after looking into the void.

The nihilism I see in most young men around me stops at the dismantling. "Life is meaningless," full stop, with no "and therefore I'll build my own meaning" afterwards: just emptiness decorated with irony, cynicism, and a detachment that feels cool at 19 and gets sadder at 25, 30, and 35.

What makes it worse is that this kind of nihilism feels like wisdom. You feel you've seen through the illusion that motivates everyone else: "A career is a hamster wheel. Relationships are temporary. All value systems are social constructs." Technically you might be right about all of it. But true and useful are different properties, and in practice, nihilism without construction works as intellectual cover for not doing anything. Indonesian Twitter calls this _masturbasi intelektual_, which is rude and correct.

The diagnostic test is simple. Does your nihilism produce anything (art, writing, action, a framework for life that you actually live by), or does it only produce reasons not to start?

### 2. No skin in the game anywhere

Taleb's skin in the game, the idea that you should bear the consequences of your own decisions, is usually read as an ethical rule. It is one, but I find it more useful as an information mechanism. People with skin in the game learn from reality; people without it learn from narrative, and a narrative can stay wrong indefinitely because nothing forces it to correct.

The weak man, in the pattern I keep seeing, has no skin in the game anywhere. He usually has a job or classes and a routine, so he looks busy. What he lacks is an arena where he actually risks something (reputation, time, emotional energy, money) with a real chance of failing and a consequence he would feel directly.

His job runs on autopilot, his relationships are low-stakes or absent, and his opinions are loud on Twitter but never tested in a room where someone can answer back. Even his moral positions are free: he posts an Instagram story about a social issue and never pays any social cost for the position.

I wrote about one version of this in "Stop The All", about the FH UI 2023 case: men posting condemnations of sexual harassment while their own group chats were anything but clean. I don't think that was an anomaly. It has the same root, which is having no domain where what you say and what you do get tested against actual consequences.

For a brutal test, look at your life and ask where failure has a price, where you can be wrong and feel the wrongness instead of just knowing it abstractly. If the answer is nowhere, you're stagnating.

### 3. The neglected body

This is the easiest one to misread.

I don't mean aesthetics. The fitness industry's six-pack standards are as distorted as the beauty industry's, and nobody needs to bench 120kg to count as strong. Bodies come in many shapes and sizes, and reducing this to appearance is a shallowness I have no interest in reproducing.

What I mean is a person's relationship with deliberate physical discomfort.

Something happens when you put your body in an uncomfortable situation on purpose: you lift something heavy, run further than is comfortable, or train a martial art where someone is literally trying to put you on the floor, and then you keep going. Nobody forces you, and usually nobody is watching. You just chose not to stop.

I think of this as pedagogy more than exercise. You're teaching your nervous system that discomfort is information rather than an order to stop, and that you can override the default that says "this is unpleasant, quit". That capacity, persisting voluntarily through discomfort, transfers to almost every other domain of life. Difficult conversations, boring work that has to be done anyway, relationships that demand emotional labour, slow and frustrating learning: all of them draw on the same base capacity to keep going when something is unpleasant.

My suspicion, and this is speculation rather than an empirical claim, is that one reason so many young men can't sustain a job, a relationship, or a project for the long run is that they've never trained this basic capacity. A body that has never been challenged produces, or at least correlates with, a psyche that has never been challenged, and a psyche that has never been challenged will always take the path of least resistance, which in almost any meaningful context leads toward irrelevance.

The data on the generational decline in young men's testosterone might be relevant here, or it might not. Travison et al. (2007) found a decline of roughly 1% per year that can't be fully explained by ageing, obesity, or measured lifestyle changes. The correlation is there and the causality isn't clear yet. Still, a hormonal decline running parallel to a behavioural one at least suggests a feedback loop between physical activity, biology, and agency.

Some of this section is autobiographical. There were periods in my life when I didn't exercise, and I felt the effect in how I handled hard things more than in the mirror. My threshold for giving up dropped, and my tolerance for discomfort shrank. That could be confirmation bias. I noted it anyway.

### 4. Consumption without production

This might be the most insidious symptom, precisely because it doesn't look like a problem.

Consuming content (social media, streaming, pornography, gaming, news, podcasts) is fine on its own; everyone does it. What matters is the ratio between what you consume and what you produce, and for a lot of the young men I know, that ratio went past dysfunctional a long time ago.

I've written about pornography specifically, about how its algorithmically optimized delivery works on the brain's reward circuitry at a scale nobody has ever tested. But pornography is one product of a bigger industry, the modern superstimulus industry.

Superstimulus is a concept from ethology, the study of animal behaviour. Niko Tinbergen found that nesting birds would neglect their own eggs to sit on a fake one that was bigger and more brightly coloured. The fake egg triggered the same instinct as the real one, only harder than anything the birds' evolutionary environment had ever produced. The birds weren't stupid. Their firmware just wasn't designed for stimulation at that level.

Pornography is a sexual superstimulus, junk food a nutritional one, social media a social one. Modern video games are an achievement superstimulus: they take the feedback loops of accomplishment, progression, and status, which in the real world play out over months or years, and compress them into hours.

None of these is evil in itself. But chronic superstimulus consumption, with no meaningful production to balance it, creates what I think is a modern version of what older generations called an empty soul: a reward system calibrated to a level of stimulation that ordinary life can't match. Work feels flat, conversation feels slow, and real relationships feel like effort next to parasocial ones that ask for nothing.

It also spirals. The more superstimulus you consume, the more your reward response to normal stimulation blunts, the more superstimulus you need to feel anything, and so on down. Kühn and Gallinat (2014) found a correlation between heavy pornography use and reduced gray matter in the right caudate nucleus, a brain region central to reward processing. Whether the consumption caused the structural change or the other way around hasn't been established. Either way, it isn't a healthy system.

Production (writing, building, making) runs on a different reward pathway. The reward is slower and smaller per unit of time, and it often comes with a healthy dose of frustration and self-doubt. But it compounds, which consumption never does. What you write today is still there tomorrow, the skill you build this month becomes the foundation for next month's, and the compound interest on consistent production is how people build anything that lasts, whether that's a career, a relationship, a body of work, or an identity.

Consumption without production, at the scale I see around me, is parasitism.

### 5. Virtue without a price

I've written about this at length elsewhere, so I'll keep it short.

There's a difference between having a virtue and displaying one, and the difference is the price.

Honesty that only shows up when it's free doesn't count for much. It becomes a virtue at the moment it costs you something (a friend, a position, your own comfort) and you say the true thing anyway. Courage works the same way: being brave in safe situations is easy, and it only becomes a virtue when the act carries real risk and you act anyway.

Weak men, in the pattern I keep seeing, have a lot of values and very few virtues. They know what's right and what's wrong in the abstract and can articulate it fluently in Instagram stories, Twitter threads, and safe conversations. When those values have to be practised at a cost to themselves, they disappear.

A group chat is making harassing jokes, and you stay quiet because pushing back would be awkward. A friend treats his partner badly, and you say nothing because "that's their business". Your workplace is corrupt, and you go along with it because "that's just how the system is". Every time you know something is wrong and choose comfort over principle, you teach yourself that your principles have a price, and that the price is low.

Here's a test I use on myself, and don't always pass: when was the last time a principle of yours actually cost you something? I mean losing something real (a friend, money, a position, a sense of safety) because you stood on what you believed was right. A mildly awkward conversation doesn't count.

If the answer is "a long time ago" or "never", I'd call what you have a preference. A preference asks nothing of you, which is exactly why it feels comfortable and exactly why it's worth so little.

I think these five (nihilism without construction, no skin in the game, the neglected body, consumption without production, and virtue without a price) are one syndrome showing up in five places. If I had to put the underlying condition in one sentence: refusing discomfort, as the default operating mode for a life.

Each symptom is a way of choosing what's easy over what's meaningful. Nihilism is easier than building a value system, and consuming is easier than producing. The same goes for staying out of every arena instead of staking something, for skipping the 5am run, and for displaying virtue instead of paying for it.

At the individual level, most of this is forgivable. Everyone has weak moments and sometimes takes the easy option; I take it more often than I'd like to admit. But when the moment becomes a collective pattern, with a large share of a generation's men running this default mode at the same time, it stops being a personal problem and becomes a systemic pollutant.

Pollution doesn't respect property lines. I'll get to what it contaminates in Section IV; first, the question of where it comes from.

## III.

If I stopped at Section II, this essay would be a longer version of the thousand "man up" sermons already on the internet, and those sermons, besides being tedious, share one fundamental problem: they never ask why.

Why does this generation of young men, specifically, show the syndrome so often? Nobody was born weaker than his grandfather, and there was no mass mutation that deleted the agency gene. Something in the environment changed, and it changed enough that the default developmental path for young men now produces a different output than it did a few decades ago.

I've asked other people for analytic empathy (understanding why someone ended up where they did without justifying where they ended up), so I owe the same courtesy here. Weak men don't need my pity, but a diagnosis without an etiology is just a label. I count five things that changed.

### A. The collapse of formative institutions

For most of human history, young men didn't become adults on their own. Institutions formed them, and those institutions were explicitly designed to channel unfocused energy into something functional. They varied across cultures, but their function was remarkably consistent.

Organized religion gave you an explicit moral framework, plus a cosmological story that placed your life inside something larger than yourself. I've written elsewhere about the limits of religious dogma and I'm not walking that back, but I can hold both thoughts at once: religion solved a real problem. It gave an answer (imperfect, sometimes dangerous, but an answer) to "what am I living for", and that question, left with no answer at all, produces the nihilism from Section II.

Apprenticeships and guilds gave you a clear trajectory. You started as a novice, learned from a master, did boring and difficult work for years, and slowly rose to become a competent craftsman. The process was slow, painful, and full of honest feedback, and at the end of it you had real skill, an identity built on competence, and a clear place in the community.

Rites of passage, from tribal initiations to compulsory military service, marked the line between boy and man. The crossing almost always involved an ordeal, something difficult, painful, or frightening that you had to walk through, and once you had walked through it, the community recognized that you'd changed: you were accountable now.

I don't want to romanticize any of this. Organized religion produced dogmatism and violence, compulsory military service is state coercion, and traditional rites of passage were often brutal and exclusionary. I don't want to go back to any of it, and my guess (I hope I'm right) is that you don't either.

The trouble is that we tore those institutions down, often for good reasons, and never built replacements. We deleted the old answers to "how does a young man become an adult" and supplied no new ones, and the answers that filled the vacuum by default have been bad: influencers selling transactional masculinity, incel forums selling resentment as an identity, and, most common and most insidious, no answer at all, just emptiness filled with superstimulus.

Ecologists know this pattern. Remove an apex predator because it's dangerous (and it really is dangerous) without understanding its regulatory role, and you get an ecosystem that collapses in ways you didn't predict: the deer population explodes, the vegetation gets stripped, the soil erodes. Taleb would call this iatrogenics, harm done by the healer. Removing something harmful without understanding the function it served just moves the problem somewhere less visible.

### B. The kafka trap of gender

I covered this in the essay on the FH UI 2023 case, so I'll be brief, but the dynamic is too important to skip.

Some gender discourse works as indoctrination rather than education, and the difference is easy to state. Education says, "there's a social pattern you need to understand, and here's how you can help change it." Indoctrination says, "you're guilty by virtue of what you are, and every response you give to this accusation proves the accusation."

Under the second version, feeling uncomfortable is your privilege talking, pushing back is fragility, staying quiet is complicity, and agreeing is fine but earns you nothing, because agreement is the baseline. Every exit is closed before you walk in.

I know this doesn't describe feminism as a whole, and I said so in "Stop The All". Most people who talk about privilege and patriarchy are making a legitimate point in a mostly reasonable way. The trouble comes from a vocal minority that runs the trap, and from the reasonable majority rarely correcting them, probably because correcting your own side costs more socially than correcting the other one.

The effect on young men is almost never sincere compliance. I see three outcomes.

The first is resentful submission: men who learn the right vocabulary (consent, toxic masculinity, intersectionality) and use it perfectly in public, then become much darker versions of themselves in closed spaces. I think that's what happened in the FH UI 2023 group chat, and it looks less like reform failing than like prohibition, which pushes behaviour underground.

The second is withdrawal: men who decide that taking part in gender discourse is too risky (anything you say can and will be used against you) and check out entirely, more out of apathy than anger. Mass apathy from half the population on questions of gender, relationships, and society is a victory for nobody.

The third is radicalization. Men who feel rejected by mainstream discourse find communities that will take them: Red Pill, MGTOW, incel forums, or worse. Those communities never ask them to feel guilty. They say "you're right and the world is wrong", a narrative that is dangerous and, to someone who has been told for years that he's categorically guilty, very appealing.

I'll repeat the caveat from "Stop The All": none of this justifies bad behaviour. Men who harass, radicalize, or walk away from social responsibility are adults with agency, and they're responsible for their choices. But fault and prevention are different questions. If the goal is prevention, we have to admit that the current discursive environment, for a significant share of young men, produces mimicry, withdrawal, or radicalization, and very little growth.

### C. A shifting labour market

This is the factor people talk about least in places like SCBD or Accounting UI, possibly because we aren't the demographic it hits.

Through most of the 20th century, men who weren't academic achievers had a clear pathway: manufacturing, construction, mining, and other physical work that paid enough to feed a family, gave you an identity built on competence ("I'm a good welder"), and gave you a clear place in your community.

Deindustrialization destroyed that pathway. In the US, manufacturing employment fell from about 30% of total employment in the 1950s to about 8% today. Indonesia is on a different timeline but moving in the same direction. As the economy shifts toward services and knowledge work, the man whose competitive advantage is physical work slowly loses economic relevance.

Meanwhile the education system, the main gateway into the knowledge economy, has become increasingly hostile to boys. Reeves summarizes the developmental research: on average, boys mature more slowly in the prefrontal regions that regulate impulse control and executive function. An education system that demands more and more sitting still, focus, and compliance (all of them neurologically harder for boys at the same age) systematically disadvantages them.

Reeves proposes something simple and radical, which is that boys start school a year later than girls to compensate for the gap in maturation. The idea, predictably, has gone almost nowhere. To many people, talking about the disadvantages boys face in education still feels like a betrayal of the fight for gender equality, as if admitting that boys can be disadvantaged in one domain cancels the admission that women are disadvantaged in another.

That's zero-sum thinking, and zero-sum thinking about gender, from any direction, is one of the most damaging failures in public discourse right now.

### D. The absence of fathers

This may be the most uncomfortable factor to discuss, because it touches nearly every assumption we hold about family structure.

Raj Chetty and his colleagues, working with tax records for millions of American children, found that the share of single-parent households in an area is among the strongest predictors of how much upward mobility children from that area get. The effect reaches beyond the single-parent families themselves: children of married parents also do worse when they grow up in areas with many single parents. A follow-up paper from the same group found that disadvantage hits boys harder. Among children from the poorest fifth of families, the usual gender gap in employment reverses and boys end up less likely to work than girls, and the variation across places is largest for boys raised in poor, single-parent families.

The most plausible mechanism, I think, is modelling. Boys learn how to be adult men largely by watching the adult men around them: how they manage anger, treat a partner, handle failure, and work. When that model is missing, boys keep looking for one somewhere else, and increasingly somewhere else means the internet, whose models of masculinity (Andrew Tate, incel forums) are, I think we can agree, not ideal.

I know that talking about absent fathers can sound like an argument for the traditional family in the conservative sense, and I don't mean it that way. Two mothers can raise an extraordinary boy, and single mothers often do it heroically. My reading of the evidence is narrower: what seems to matter is the presence of a functional adult male role model (a father, an uncle, a teacher, a mentor, anyone), and the absence of that model, which is increasingly common, has consequences that are becoming just as common. The data can't cleanly separate this from income and neighbourhood effects, so treat the role-model part as my interpretation.

### E. The superstimulus environment

This is the factor closest to your own experience as a reader, so I won't need to explain much.

You live in an environment that, for the first time in the species' history, offers unlimited access to high-intensity stimulation at close to zero cost: free pornography, cheap gaming, free social media, nearly free streaming, all of it delivered through a device in your pocket, available twenty-four hours a day, and tuned by machine-learning systems whose only objective is to maximize the time you spend on the platform.

I've written about the mechanism in the pornography essay, and the principle generalizes. Human reward circuitry evolved in environments where rewards were scarce and cost effort: food meant hunting or gathering, sex meant social bonds, entertainment meant other people, and status meant real contribution.

Now all of those rewards are available for almost no effort, and a reward system designed for scarcity handles abundance badly. Biologists call this evolutionary mismatch, and I think it's a better explanation than bad character. The bird on the fake egg wasn't stupid, and the young man who spends six hours a day on TikTok isn't morally inferior; both are running firmware that wasn't built for input at that level.

But knowing you're vulnerable to something doesn't give you permission to surrender to it. Unlike the birds, we have a prefrontal cortex, and with it the capacity to recognize patterns, understand mechanisms, and make choices that override our defaults. Using that capacity is expensive and willpower is finite, but the capacity exists. Once you know what's happening, refusing to use it stops being mismatch and becomes a choice.

Put the five together (collapsing formative institutions, the kafka trap in gender discourse, a shifting labour market, absent role models, and a superstimulus environment) and you get conditions in which weakness is the path of least resistance for young men.

Does that make weak men victims? I don't think so. All five factors are real, and they make becoming strong harder than it was for earlier generations, or at least hard in a different way. I'm not going to minimize that; environment and starting conditions matter.

But everyone faces constraints and lives in an environment they didn't choose. Women historically faced constraints far heavier than anything I've described, and as a group they now outperform men on almost every educational metric and on a growing list of professional ones. Constraints are context, and nobody gets a pass because of them.

Context matters for a practical reason: it lets us design interventions that actually work. "Man up" is a slogan, and slogans don't change environments. What changes them is an understanding of the mechanism precise enough that we can modify the mechanism instead of shouting at the output.

Before getting to solutions, there's one more question to answer: why is male weakness your problem, and not just a problem for the men who have it?

## IV.

It's tempting to treat the weak man as a self-contained problem: "let him be, he's the only one who suffers." I understand the impulse and sometimes feel it myself. If weakness really were self-contained, if a weak man only damaged himself, I might not have written this essay. Liberty says people have the right to ruin their own lives as long as they don't harm anyone else.

The trouble is that weak men do harm other people. They do it without intending to and without any plan, the way air pollution does: as a byproduct of behaviour that feels, to the person doing it, entirely personal and unconnected to anyone else.

Economists call this a negative externality, a cost of an activity that falls on people who didn't take part in it. The factory dumping waste into the river keeps the profit, and the community downstream gets mercury in its drinking water. The smoker gets the nicotine, and the person sitting next to him gets the smoke.

A weak man, in aggregate, is a factory dumping waste into five rivers at once.

### Externality 1. Distorting the romance market

I wrote about this at length in "Like a Deep-Fried Stock", so I'll summarize the main argument and add a layer I didn't articulate at the time.

The romance market, like any market, needs working price discovery, and price discovery needs a feedback loop. Unrealistic expectations should run into the consequence that nobody is willing to meet them, and that consequence should force recalibration. Weak men break the loop.

The Permanent Standby, waiting on someone who has explicitly not made him a priority, sends a false signal that attention without reciprocity is sustainable. The Unconditional Subsidizer, giving emotional support, resources, and time to someone with no intention of reciprocating, artificially inflates the recipient's sense of her market value. The Desperate Settler, accepting conditions objectively below standard because he's afraid of being alone, tells the market that low standards still produce committed partners.

This is basic market mechanics. When the supply of male attention never falls, whatever the conditions, prices can't be discovered, and when prices can't be discovered, everyone operates on wrong information about their own position. That includes the men who aren't subsidizing anyone, who now compete in a market whose expectations were inflated by other men's behaviour.

What I didn't say at the time is that the distortion harms women too. A woman whose sense of her market value has been inflated by unconditional subsidy will, mechanically, turn down partners who are actually sekufu with her, because next to a distorted baseline they feel like settling. The result is that women who could be in functional, fulfilling relationships keep looking for something that, statistically, doesn't exist in their bracket, and often realize it only after years of their best window have gone.

Women with high standards aren't the source of this distortion, since high standards are rational in any market, and the dating apps only accelerate dynamics that already existed. The distortion comes from men who keep giving away something valuable (time, attention, resources, commitment) and ask for nothing in return: weak men acting as unconditional subsidizers, a floor that keeps falling.

### Externality 2. Contaminating public discourse

In "Stop The All" I wrote about how virtue signalling contaminates the public conversation about gender, but I didn't connect it to this syndrome.

A man who posts an Instagram story about rape culture while his own group chat is filthy is showing the fifth symptom, virtue without a price. A man with no skin in the game anywhere, whose default is to avoid discomfort, drifts naturally toward the cheapest available form of "contribution", and nothing is cheaper than a post.

Performative discourse is the smaller cost. The bigger one is that discourse becomes unfalsifiable. When the loudest voices on a gender issue belong to the people with the least skin in the game, what they say never gets tested against reality, and untested claims can only escalate. The motte drifts into the bailey, the reasonable claim into the absolutist one, and "there's a pattern that needs fixing" turns into "Kill All Men". Nobody involved has to be evil for this to happen. There's simply no correction mechanism, no skin in the game forcing the claims to face consequences.

Two groups pay for this. The first is women who are fighting for legitimate change. Every time a hypocritical man borrows the feminist narrative for social capital, he dilutes the credibility of the movement he claims to support, and once people start suspecting that "male feminist" is a performance, the suspicion doesn't stay on the performers. It lands on everyone who uses the same language, sincere people included.

The second is young men who are trying to understand and improve themselves. They look at the contaminated discourse and conclude one of two things. Either it's all bullshit and nobody is sincere, which pushes them toward cynicism and withdrawal, or they'd better post the same things to stay socially safe, which produces one more virtue signaller. Neither produces a better man, and both feed the loop.

### Externality 3. Funding an exploitative industry

In the pornography essay I argued that the traditional industry is what Taleb calls a Bob Rubin trade: executives take the profit and performers take the risk. What I didn't connect there is the demand side.

An exploitative industry can't exist without demand, and the demand for pornography at industrial scale (free, unlimited, and consumed in volumes that would cause a moral panic if the substance were anything psychoactive) comes disproportionately from one demographic: young men whose consumption far exceeds their production, whose reward systems are calibrated to superstimulus, and who have no other source of intimacy or meaningful achievement.

This is supply and demand, and I'm not trying to moralize it. When millions of young men spend hours that could have built something (a skill, a relationship, a contribution) on content from an industry with documented structural problems, they damage their own reward circuitry, and they also keep alive an economic model that harms the most vulnerable people inside it.

PornHub hosted child exploitation material for years and only acted when Visa and Mastercard threatened to cut off its payment processing. What let it ignore the problem for that long was traffic, hundreds of millions of visits a day. That traffic is the demand, and the demand comes overwhelmingly from men.

Saying "I'm only watching, I'm not hurting anyone" is like saying "I'm only buying, the factory conditions have nothing to do with me". As a statement about you personally, it might be true. As a statement about the system, it's wilful blindness: you are the demand, and without demand the supply isn't viable.

### Externality 4. Radicalization and political instability

This is the externality people least associate with "weak men", and maybe the most dangerous at scale.

I can't think of a significant modern extremist movement that wasn't built from the same raw material, directionless and resentful young men. ISIS and the alt-right both recruited from that population, and the incel movement is that population organizing itself.

What people underestimate is how short the pipeline from "ordinary, slightly lost young man" to "radicalized young man" has become. Online radicalization can take months. Recommendation algorithms on YouTube, TikTok, and the rest, optimized for engagement and indifferent to truth, efficiently walk young men from mildly edgy content into openly extremist content, from self-improvement to looksmaxxing to inceldom to white supremacy.

Men with purpose, community, and a stake in society are hard to recruit; they're busy, invested, and have too much to lose. The pipeline works on men who have none of that: the nihilists, the isolated, the men with no skin in the game anywhere, the men who feel the world has no place for them. In other words, weak men, and what makes them recruitable is emptiness more than malice. Any sufficiently savvy operator can fill a vacuum.

When a directionless young man goes down the rabbit hole and comes out an extremist, that's an individual tragedy and also a threat to everyone around him. And the number of men sitting at the entrance of that pipeline (nihilistic, isolated, angry, purposeless) is, I'd guess, larger today than at any point in modern history.

### Externality 5. The extra burden carried by women

This is the most counterintuitive of the five, and possibly the most important for this essay's audience.

The standard frame says patriarchy harms women and men benefit, which is true in aggregate and historically. At the interpersonal, present-day level, though, something more specific is going on: weak men create an extra burden that falls disproportionately on the women around them. It takes several forms.

The first is emotional labour without reciprocity. A woman partnered with a man who has never consciously processed his emotional life (no vocabulary for his feelings, withdrawal or anger as his default response to distress, no habit of introspection) becomes his unpaid therapist. The issue is capacity rather than romance: he lacks the baseline emotional capacity to take part in a relationship as an equal, and the work of compensating for that almost always falls on her.

The second is the market distortion from Externality 1. When male subsidy inflates perceived market value, women operate on wrong information and turn down partners who are actually compatible (sekufu), because the baseline is distorted. By the time reality corrects, through age, life stage, or accumulated experience, the correction is often late and painful.

The third, and the starkest, is children raised without a functional co-parent. A man without agency over his own life can't be one. When parenting falls disproportionately on the mother, because the father is absent or because he's present in body and absent in function, calling it "family dynamics" hides what it is: an externality paid by women and children.

The fourth, and the bleakest, is physical safety. Most violence against women is committed by men they know. It has many causes, but one consistent thread is men who can't manage emotion constructively, especially anger and frustration: men whose default response to emotional discomfort is discharge instead of processing, and whose discharge often lands, violently, on the people closest to them and most vulnerable.

I know that paragraph is heavy, and I wrote it carefully. Many weak men aren't violent at all; they're withdrawn rather than aggressive. But the overlap between the syndrome I've been describing (inability to handle discomfort, poor emotional regulation, nihilism) and the risk profile for domestic violence is large enough that leaving it out would be dishonest.

Five rivers, then, contaminated by a single source: the romance market, public discourse, an exploitative industry, radicalization, and the burden carried by women.

In every one of them, the people paying the cost are mostly different from the people producing the pollution. The weak man bears some of the consequences, and his own life is the most damaged, but most of the bill goes to women, children, communities, and society at large. It's a textbook negative externality and a textbook Bob Rubin trade: he takes the path of least resistance and other people pay.

That makes it a public problem, and public problems need more than "fix yourself" advice. They need change at the individual level and at the level of the system.

## V.

I don't like giving advice, and this section is going to sound a lot like advice, damn. There's something epistemically suspicious about a man in his early twenties telling other people how to live. But I've spent four sections on diagnosis, and stopping there would turn this into a longer complaint, so I'll try, with the caveat that I don't always practise everything below and that some of it might be wrong.

A second caveat: this is aimed at men who recognized themselves in Section II, the ones who read it and thought "damn, some of that is me". If you read Section II and recognized nothing at all, either you're further along than I assumed or you're in a denial that will take more than an essay to break. I can't tell which from here.

### 1. Find one domain where you have skin in the game

Just one. I'm emphasizing this on purpose, because one of the most common failure modes of self-improvement is trying to change everything at once, which is a reliable recipe for quitting within two weeks. Willpower isn't infinite, and every extra domain you take on drains the same reservoir.

By a domain with skin in the game I mean an arena where failure has a price you actually feel, success takes effort you actually spend, and other people can judge the result.

It could be almost anything: a competitive sport where you lose in front of people; a small business where you stake real time and money; a technical skill whose output is visible and can be criticized (coding, writing, cooking, carpentry, music production, anything that produces a real output); a community project where other people depend on you for an outcome. Even a committed relationship counts, since you're explicitly staking time, emotional energy, and the possibility of heartbreak.

Things that don't count: watching Zahid Ibrahim and Ali Abdaal's self-improvement videos (ironic, given that you're reading this essay), binging CBum's YouTube without going to the gym like he does, holding strong opinions online without ever defending them in a room where people can answer back, and planning without executing. Planning is where most people get stuck, because it feels like doing. A to-do list isn't skin in the game, and crossing items off one might not be either, depending on whether the item could have failed.

The simplest test is whether there's a scenario in which the outcome could embarrass you. If there is, you have skin in the game. If there isn't, you're still in the safe zone, and the safe zone, as I've argued, is where weakness compounds.

Start with one and make it non-negotiable. Add another only when you have the bandwidth.

### 2. Do something physically hard, regularly

If you've never exercised consistently, I'd start with resistance training. Running, swimming, and martial arts all have good arguments behind them, but resistance training has a few properties that make it an especially good entry point for someone who has never built a physical habit.

The feedback loop is fast and unambiguous. You lift 40kg this week and 42.5kg next week, and you know progress happened, because the number either went up or it didn't. For someone whose life is full of ambiguity and short on measurable progress, that clarity is therapeutic.

The barrier to entry is low. You don't need athletic talent, coordination, or teammates, just a barbell and gravity, and both are widely available.

And the discomfort is graduated and controllable: you start light and add weight in small steps. That matters, because too much discomfort too fast produces avoidance instead of adaptation.

There's one more effect I can't explain mechanically but see too consistently to ignore. Men who start lifting regularly almost always report a change in how they handle things outside the gym: difficult conversations feel a little less terrifying, boring tasks a little more manageable, and the threshold for giving up rises. It might be hormonal (resistance training does raise testosterone acutely), or psychological (repeated evidence that you can endure discomfort changes your model of yourself), or both. I don't know the mechanism, but the output is consistent enough that I'd recommend it anyway.

If you already have a physical habit, good, but ask whether it still challenges you. A 5K you can run half-asleep is maintenance by now, and maintenance, while better than nothing, doesn't produce adaptation. Adaptation needs progressive overload, periodically raising the stimulus above what already feels comfortable. The principle holds in the gym, and I suspect it holds in life.

### 3. Cut the superstimulus, gradually

This is the prescription that's easiest to say and hardest to do, so it has to be realistic.

I won't tell you to delete all social media, stop gaming, and never watch pornography again starting today. That's cold turkey, and cold turkey has a low success rate for almost any addictive or entrenched behaviour.

What works better is making consumption mechanically harder and production mechanically easier. Delete the apps from your home screen and keep the accounts. Add friction: app timers, grayscale mode, the phone in another room while you work. Then change the default for when you're bored. Going from Instagram to academic journals is too big a jump, but going from Instagram to a book, or from four hours of gaming to two hours plus an hour of coding, is doable.

On pornography specifically, the full argument is in the other essay. Here I'll only say this: if you consume it regularly, try stopping for thirty days and see what happens. Treat it as an experiment rather than a moral test. Notice whether anything changes in your energy, your motivation, the way you see the people around you, or the quality of your social interactions. If nothing changes, fair enough. If something does (and many people who've tried it report that something does), you'll have personal data more valuable than any argument I could write.

One rule: don't announce it. Don't post about NoFap or your digital detox. The moment you announce a behaviour change publicly, you collect the social reward for intending it without having executed anything, and reward without execution is exactly the pattern you're trying to break. There's some experimental evidence for this, too: Peter Gollwitzer's studies found that people who announced their goals followed through less than people who kept quiet.

### 4. Produce something, anything, regularly

I'm listing this separately from skin in the game because the two overlap without being the same. You can produce something without skin in the game (a private journal nobody reads), and you can have skin in the game without producing anything (gambling, for instance: real risk, no creation).

What I mean here is a regular habit of making something that wasn't there before: writing, code, music, a meal, a design, anything you can point at afterwards and say, "this wasn't here this morning, and now it is, because of me."

The reason is mechanical more than motivational. Production is the most direct antidote to nihilism. Nihilism says nothing is meaningful, and production answers with evidence instead of counter-philosophy: this exists now, and it didn't before I made it. You don't need to argue about meaning while you're busy making some.

I think this is what Nietzsche meant, though I'm not sure: meaning isn't lying around waiting to be found, you have to make it, and you make it by filling your life with output you stake and care about.

I'm not recommending hustle culture, being productive every waking second, which is a different pathology; workaholism is a superstimulus dressed up as virtue. What I care about is the ratio. Does the net flow of your life tilt toward consumption or toward production? If the answer is "consumption, and not by a little", fix the allocation.

### 5. Hold principles that are falsifiable, and test them

I wrote about values versus virtues in Section II. Here's another layer: some principles are falsifiable and some aren't.

An unfalsifiable principle sounds nice and demands nothing. "I believe in honesty" is lovely until someone asks when your honesty last cost you anything, and "I value loyalty" is lovely until someone asks whether you've ever stayed loyal when betrayal would have paid better.

A falsifiable principle has observable consequences, so someone watching from outside could tell whether you actually hold it. "I will not stay silent when my friend treats someone badly" is falsifiable: specific moments test it, and you can pass or fail. "I believe all people deserve respect" is unfalsifiable, because there's no situation in which you could visibly fail to hold it; the statement is too abstract to predict anything about your behaviour.

A useful exercise: write down three principles you hold. For each one, write (a) the most recent concrete situation in which it was tested and (b) what you did. If you can't fill in (a), the principle has never been tested, and you don't yet know whether it's really yours or just an aspiration. If you can fill in (a) but (b) is disappointing, now you know where the gap is. This is homework. Please do it.

Nobody holds all their principles all the time, and I've failed in places I'm ashamed to describe. The useful question is whether you know where you failed and intend to do differently next time. That's what separates someone who has a principle and is still debugging the implementation from someone whose values were only ever decorative.

### 6. Apply sekufu to yourself first

In "Like a Deep-Fried Stock" I wrote about sekufu as a framework for choosing a partner: looking for equality in epistemology, values, trajectory, and emotional capacity. I still hold that argument.

But there's a step I skipped in that essay and should have made explicit: sekufu demands an honest self-assessment that most people aren't ready to perform.

Saying "I'll only settle for someone equal to me" assumes you know who you are, in the operational sense rather than the abstract "I'm a good person, fairly smart, reasonably hard-working" sense. Where do you actually sit on each dimension?

Your epistemology: can you really change your mind when you're given new evidence, or are you the kind of person who feels rational but is mostly good at rationalizing conclusions he already holds? How many times in the last year have you changed your position on something important?

Your values, meaning the ones you reveal through behaviour rather than the ones you claim: you say you value honesty, so how many times did you lie (lies of omission included) in the past week? You say you believe in hard work, so what do you do when the work is unpleasant and nobody is watching?

Your trajectory: where are you going, in the actual sense rather than the LinkedIn sense? Is your life, today, moving in the direction you want, or are you drifting, with the career goal serving as decoration over the fact that you don't know where you want to go?

Your emotional capacity: can you sit with an uncomfortable emotion without immediately discharging it by getting angry, withdrawing, blaming someone, or self-medicating with superstimulus? Can you listen to someone talk about something painful without rushing to fix it or change the subject? Have you ever cried in front of another person? Do you know why you feel what you feel, or do feelings just happen to you, illegibly?

These questions are uncomfortable by design; a comfortable version would be useless.

The point is calibration, and self-flagellation doesn't help with it. You want to know your real position so that you can (a) close the gap between where you are and where you want to be, and (b) look for a partner who is equal to who you are now, as opposed to the idealized version of yourself.

This is where I see things fail most often: men who want a partner with a sophisticated epistemology, strong values, an ambitious trajectory, and mature emotional capacity, who themselves haven't met that standard on a single one of the four dimensions. I don't think that's sekufu. That's wishful thinking, or as we'd say, _ngarep_.

"Fix yourself first" is unsexy advice, and it's mechanically necessary: you can't judge equality without knowing your own position, and you can't ask of someone else what you can't offer yourself.

One last thing about the list above: read as a whole, it's overwhelming. Find skin in the game, exercise, cut superstimulus, produce something, hold real principles, calibrate yourself; that's a lot, and if you've spent a long time in the default mode from Section II (nihilistic, passive, consumption-heavy, with no skin in the game), the distance between you and all of it feels absurd.

I don't have a fix for that feeling, only a suggestion: look at the first step instead of the distance. The first step is always small and unimpressive, but it changes your trajectory, and over a long enough horizon, trajectory matters more than position.

You don't need to be a different person tomorrow, just a person who is moving in the right direction tomorrow, even slightly, even if nobody sees it. Especially if nobody sees it, since that's how you tell change from performance.

## VI.

"Man up" assumes the problem is entirely individual: weak men are weak because they choose to be, and the fix is choosing otherwise. In Section III I argued that this is partly true, since agency and choice are real, and also that the choices get made inside an environment that systematically pushes toward weakness. Environments change when systems are redesigned, and individuals choosing better won't redesign them.

An analogy that might help: if 30% of a city's population is obese, you can blame each individual for eating too much and moving too little, and you'd be technically right, since each of them is making that choice. But stopping there misses the more important question, which is why 30% of the population is making the same choice. Maybe something about the environment (food deserts, car-dependent infrastructure, processed-food marketing, working hours that leave no time to cook) has made the bad choice the default and the good choice an effort.

Obesity is an individual problem and a systemic one, and so is male weakness.

### 1. Stop using the kafka trap in gender discourse

I've covered this in "Stop The All" and in Section III, but it needs saying once more as a prescription: how we talk to young men about masculinity matters, and the current approach isn't working.

Telling someone "you're guilty because of who you are, and every response you give proves it" produces one of three outputs, all bad: false compliance, withdrawal, or radicalization. None of them produces better men.

What seems to work, as far as I can tell from the evidence available, is an approach that acknowledges the problem honestly without making the entire group its cause. Compare "there's a behavioural pattern that does harm, and you may be taking part in it without realizing; let's look at it together" with "you, as a man, are part of an oppressive system, and your privilege keeps you from seeing it".

The first framing invites introspection and the second invites self-defence, and you can't do both at once, since they draw on the same cognitive resources and pull them in opposite directions.

My argument here is about effectiveness, and it has nothing to do with being softer on men. If the goal is to change behaviour (as opposed to expressing anger), a framing that produces defensiveness is self-sabotage that happens to feel satisfying.

And for men who already take part in this discourse: stop using language you don't understand just because it's socially safe. If you post about dismantling patriarchy but can't define patriarchy in a falsifiable way, you're adding noise, and noise drowns out the signal from the people doing the hard work.

### 2. Build new formative institutions

In Section III I described how we tore down the institutions that used to form young men (organized religion, apprenticeship, rites of passage) and never built replacements. The obvious prescription is to build them.

The goal would be new institutions that serve the same function as the old ones (structure, accountability, progression, community) without the baggage that made the old ones worth tearing down. Nobody needs to bring back compulsory military service for this. A few such institutions already exist and, I think, work, just not yet at sufficient scale.

_Structured mentorship programmes_ deliberately pair young men with functional adult men, so that having a mentor doesn't depend on the luck of whom you happen to know. Reeves emphasizes this, especially for boys growing up without a father figure, and programmes like Big Brothers Big Sisters, while imperfect, show measurable benefits for mentees: better school attendance, less antisocial behaviour, more self-efficacy.

_Martial arts gyms and serious athletic communities_ are places with a competence-based hierarchy, where seniors teach juniors and people face a physical challenge together (a gym where everyone wears headphones and nobody talks doesn't count). A well-run BJJ gym, boxing club, or muay thai camp is functionally a modern rite of passage. You arrive as a beginner who knows nothing, you get humbled regularly, you slowly build competence, and all along you're surrounded by people who have already been through what you're going through. It's a formative institution that happens to be located in a gym.

_Vocational training and trade schools, without the stigma._ University isn't for everyone, and plenty of people don't thrive in academic settings. For many young men, especially those whose competitive advantage is hands-on work, a path through vocational training (welding, mechanics, electrical work) can deliver exactly what they need: real skill, an identity built on competence, a decent income, and skin in the game. In Germany and Switzerland, dual-education systems that combine apprenticeship with formal schooling consistently produce good outcomes. I can't see a structural reason why Indonesia, or anywhere else, couldn't adopt something similar. The obstacles are mostly cultural, and culture can change.

_Project-based communities_, meaning hackathons, maker spaces, art collectives, and open-source projects, are places where people show up to make something together, as opposed to socializing in the abstract. Communities built on shared production tend to be sturdier than communities built on shared consumption or shared identity, because production gives them objective common ground that doesn't depend on everyone liking each other.

### 3. Education reform that admits the gender gap

I'll put this bluntly: boys are failing in our education systems, and almost nobody wants to talk about it.

The data from Section I, that women outperform men at almost every level of education in almost every OECD country, isn't new. The gap has been opening for decades, and the policy attention it gets is close to zero, especially next to the (legitimate) attention paid to gaps that disadvantage women in STEM, in leadership, and elsewhere.

My suspicion is that admitting boys can be disadvantaged in one domain still feels, to many people, like betraying the admission that women are disadvantaged in others. That's the zero-sum thinking I criticized above, and it does just as much damage here. Women face structural barriers in many domains, and boys are falling behind in education; both are true. Acknowledging the second doesn't cancel the first, and refusing to acknowledge it for fear that it might is dogma. I thought we had agreed to leave dogma behind.

A few interventions have some evidence behind them.

Reeves proposes that boys start school a year later than girls, sometimes called redshirting, based on the developmental data showing that boys mature more slowly in the brain regions that matter most for academic success. It's simple and cheap, Reeves makes a decent evidence-based case for it, and it has gone almost nowhere.

Hire more male teachers, especially in primary school. In many countries the share of male primary teachers is very low, in some places under 15%. There's documented correlation between the presence of male teachers and boys' academic performance, though the causal story needs more research. At minimum it's a variable we can intervene on, so we could try.

Design curriculum and pedagogy for more variation in how children learn: more hands-on work, more physical movement, more structured competition, and less sitting still for seven hours. None of this has to come at girls' expense. The point is that one-size-fits-all pedagogy, which keeps becoming more dominant, systematically fails to fit most boys.

### 4. Normalize corrective feedback among men

In "Like a Deep-Fried Stock" I described how female friendships can become echo chambers that never deliver corrective feedback. Men have an echo chamber too, differently shaped and just as damaging, and its shape is avoidance.

If women's friendships tend to over-validate, men's tend to under-engage. Male friendship often runs on an unspoken contract, "I won't comment on your life and you won't comment on mine", which feels like respect and is closer to negligence.

When did you last say to a male friend, "Dul, I think you're wrecking your life", or "the way you treat your girlfriend isn't right", or "you haven't done anything meaningful in six months and I'm worried"?

If the answer is never, and for most men I suspect it is, then you're a comfortable friend, which in the end is the friend who watches someone he loves drown from the edge of the pool, saying "well, that's his business".

There are reasons men avoid these conversations. They're awkward, they can damage the friendship, and they can be met with defensiveness or anger. All true, and that's exactly why such a conversation is one of the most valuable things you can give someone: it's costly, and you're taking on discomfort in someone else's interest. That makes it feedback and also virtue in the sense from Section II, something you do at a cost to yourself.

I doubt a campaign will normalize this. More likely it happens through individual men deciding to stop being bystanders in their friends' lives, one honest conversation at a time, each time choosing to say what needs saying over what's safe to say.

### 5. Regulate superstimulus for adolescents

This is the most policy-oriented prescription and probably the most controversial, so I'll try to be precise about what I'm proposing.

First, what I'm not proposing: a ban on social media or gaming for adults. As I argued in the pornography essay, prohibition pushes behaviour underground and makes things worse for the most vulnerable, and adults have the right to make bad decisions about their own lives. We believe in liberty, right?

What I am proposing is meaningful barriers between adolescents and superstimulus that their reward circuitry isn't ready for.

Start by regulating algorithmic recommendation for minors. Recommendation engines are optimized for engagement, which mechanically means optimized for content that triggers the strongest emotional response: outrage, anxiety, arousal, envy. For an adult brain with a mature prefrontal cortex, that's annoying. For an adolescent brain whose prefrontal cortex is still under construction, it's potentially formative in ways we don't yet understand. Taleb's precautionary principle applies here: when we don't know the long-term effects and the downside is potentially large and hard to reverse, the default should lean toward restriction.

The obvious counterargument is that parents should supervise. Ideally, yes. In the real world most parents aren't digitally savvy enough to understand what their kids consume, let alone manage it, and handing the entire responsibility to parents you know lack the tools is abdication.

I'm aware that this section, on what society needs to do, is much more tentative than Section V. Individual prescriptions are easier because the scope is small and the feedback loop is fast: start going to a BJJ gym and you'll feel the effect within weeks. Systemic prescriptions are harder because they involve coordination problems, political will, real trade-offs, and messy implementation.

Still, I think naming what needs to change has value even before the how is clear. And if I had to pick the one item above with the largest impact per unit of effort, it would be normalizing corrective feedback among men. Its advantage is that anyone can start it today, without waiting for policy change, education reform, or political will that may never arrive. All it takes is caring enough about a friend to say what he doesn't want to hear, and being strong enough to absorb the discomfort of the conversation.

You may have noticed that this is an individual prescription dressed up as a systemic one. Maybe that's the point. Systemic change might, in the end, start with individual decisions made by enough people at once that they become a norm, in which case top-down and bottom-up meet at the same place: a person, at a particular moment, deciding to do the hard thing.

## VII.

I've spent six sections criticizing weak men with what I hope is enough precision. I've defined them, explained how they got this way, laid out the damage they cause, and offered prescriptions for how they, and the system around them, can change.

Now for the question I find least comfortable: how much of this is about me?

Nihilism without construction: there was a period in my life, longer than I'd like to admit, when I used cynicism as a substitute for a personality, and "everything is meaningless" was less a philosophical conclusion than a justification for not trying. Am I fully out of that phase? I hope so, and hoping is weaker than knowing.

Skin in the game: I'm writing this essay, and people will read it and judge it, so there's some. But criticizing weak men in front of an audience that will mostly applaud isn't high-risk behaviour. The real test is being strong in practice, in the moments nobody watches and in the small choices that can't be turned into an essay.

The body: I exercise, but is it challenging, or am I in a maintenance zone that feels productive while staying comfortable? It depends on the week.

Consumption and production: I write, and I also consume more than I want to admit, five-minute scrolls that become forty-five and nights lost to content that adds nothing. The ratio beats zero. Whether it's good, I'm not sure.

Virtue with a price: I write about honesty and principle, and there are conversations I should have had with friends and didn't, because they would have been awkward. There were moments when I knew something was wrong and stayed silent because pushing back felt too expensive. By my own definition that's virtue without a price, so by my own standard I'm failing my own test.

The most uncomfortable question is whether this essay is itself a substitute for the harder work.

An essay this long about weak men is satisfying to write. There's dopamine in feeling that you've identified the problem precisely, in feeling above the situation you're describing, and in feeling that writing about weakness is evidence of strength. That dopamine isn't very different from what I criticized in men who post Instagram stories about social issues and never do anything about them.

Is it more than an Instagram story? I think so: there's real analytic effort and research in it, and an argument that has to hold together across thousands of words. Whether it meets the standard for skin in the game that I set for myself is another matter, and I suspect the answer is "not yet".

I ended "Stop The All" by admitting that an essay criticizing virtue signalling can itself be signalling, of intellectual superiority instead of moral superiority. I wrote that maybe nobody is above this game, and that the best we can do is notice that we're playing it.

This essay sits in the same predicament, maybe more deeply.

Here's what is actually happening: a man not yet 22, still in the middle of becoming whatever he'll become, is writing a long essay criticizing other men for failing to become what they should. There's something almost comically presumptuous about that. What's my qualification, beyond being able to write like this?

And I know perfectly well that part of my motivation is less than altruistic. Part of it is wanting to feel that I'm not the weak man I'm describing: define the category, describe it in detail, and quietly place myself outside it. That's an identity built on negation ("I'm not them"), and identities built on negation are fragile, because they need a "them" to function.

It's the same regress I ran into in "Stop The All": every layer of self-awareness can be another layer of performance. Saying "I know I'm signalling" is signalling, and so is saying "I know my self-awareness is signalling", all the way down.

I don't have a solution to the regress, and I'm not sure one exists. Maybe the best we can do is pick a level at which to stop, and act from that level as honestly as we can, while admitting that the honesty itself might be a performance we can't see from the inside.

There's one thing, though, that I don't think is performance, or if it is, it's costly enough that it probably isn't worth faking: the discomfort you're feeling right now.

If you're a man, you've read this far, and parts of it made your stomach turn a little (the part about nihilism without construction, maybe, or consumption without production, or virtue without a price), that turning is data. It's a feedback loop doing its job, reality knocking and asking whether this is about you. For most of us, me included, the answer is partly yes.

Everyone has been weak at some point, so that question isn't very interesting. The interesting one is whether you'll stay weak: whether the weakness becomes an identity, something you defend and rationalize and build a life on, or a data point, something you acknowledge, understand the mechanism of, and use as a starting point to move somewhere else.

I don't know which way you'll go, and I don't always know which way I'll go. What I do know is that this essay, with all its caveats and self-doubt and infinite regress, is written on the premise that people can change: that a man who recognizes himself in Section II today can, tomorrow or next month, start becoming someone who no longer does. That's a hypothesis, and like any hypothesis it gets tested by acting. Reading doesn't test it.

So close this tab, put the phone down, and do one thing you've known for a while you need to do and haven't done, because it was uncomfortable. Today, if not right now.

If you actually do it (as opposed to thinking "yeah, later" and scrolling to the next piece of content), then maybe this essay turns out to be more than a longer Instagram story. I'm not sure, but I'm willing to bet on it, which is, I suppose, a small amount of skin in the game.
