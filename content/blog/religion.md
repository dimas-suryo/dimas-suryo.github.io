_Maybe it's time to start questioning an algorithm that has gone obsolete._

_Epistemic status: the origin story in Section I is the one I find most plausible, but it's contested, and I flag where the evidence is thin. The argument in the later sections doesn't depend on it._

## I.

### It was the best shield available

Imagine you're a behavioural economist who has been asked to design a civilization from scratch. The problem statement is fairly clean: you have to coordinate ten thousand selfish primates whose default loyalty is to their own families, and get them to cooperate at scale, without centralized oversight and without any meaningful enforcement. You have ten thousand years.

Modern behavioural science has existed for about fifty years. Our ancestors, without game theory, without Kahneman, and without Elinor Ostrom, solved this problem with what I think is one of the most elegant pieces of cognitive engineering in human history.

Anthropology gives us Dunbar's number: a cognitive limit, somewhere around 150 people, on the number of stable relationships a primate brain can maintain. (The number itself is contested; a reanalysis put its 95% confidence interval anywhere from 4 to 520. But some limit clearly exists, and the argument only needs that.) Past that limit, social cohesion falls apart, because gossip and communal memory aren't enough to keep free riders from chewing through the commons.

The solution was stories about the heavens: invisible beings watching every move (cosmic CCTV, if you like), plus the reward of heaven and the eternal punishment of hell, which together calibrate the incentive structure more effectively than any police force could. Add calendars and rituals that synchronize people in time, and you get fifty thousand strangers praying at the same hour, fasting on the same day, and facing the same direction.

That was arguably the earliest distributed consensus protocol humanity ever ran, and a clever one. I should say that this is the story I find most plausible, and it isn't a settled result. Ara Norenzayan's "Big Gods" argument, that moralizing gods made large-scale cooperation possible, has serious critics, and whether moralizing gods came before large societies or after them is still argued over.

Some of the data fits, with caveats. Robert Putnam and David Campbell's survey work in _American Grace_ found that regular churchgoers give and volunteer more than other Americans, and that the social networks built in congregations, more than theology, explain most of the difference. In a large study of American nurses, women who attended religious services at least weekly had a suicide rate roughly five times lower than women who never attended (VanderWeele et al., 2016), though that's observational. The experimental evidence is shakier: the well-known finding that religious primes make people more generous in economic games has failed to replicate.

Religion was also the original social safety net. Churches fed the hungry long before BPJS, pesantren taught children for free long before UNICEF, and monasteries tended people who would otherwise have died alone long before anyone said "hospice". For hundreds of millions of people today, this is still how things work.

Even Nietzsche, who announced the death of God, expected it to bring on a wave of nihilism, and I don't think he ever offered a satisfying way out. As far as I know, neither did Camus. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy" reads, to me, like the answer of someone who has accepted that his cosmos is meaningless but still has to find a reason to get out of bed.

So here's a claim worth saying out loud: anyone who says religion is just stupidity and a con hasn't thought about it long enough. Religion is one of the most cost-effective solutions humanity has found to its coordination problem, and its good consequences are real and still happening.

## II.

### One problem that's hard to ignore

The trouble with brilliant legacy software is that it has no update button.

When the software written to bind a hundred and fifty people in a desert village gets forked, cloned, and ported onto hardware that includes nuclear reactors, ballistic missiles, and genome editing, the output can be worse than suboptimal. It can be dangerous. I'm not claiming it definitely is, only that the risk deserves to be taken seriously.

### Glitch uno: when compromise becomes sin

{{< figure src="/images/blog/religion/image2.jpeg" alt="Bobby Fischer vs Mikhail Tal in Leipzig Olympiad (1960)" caption="<a href=&quot;https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ABezr4s10sc&quot;>Bobby Fischer vs Mikhail Tal in Leipzig Olympiad (1960)</a>" >}}

If you peel every religious label off the Israel-Palestine conflict, what you find at the centre is a property dispute, a fight over land and resources. In the most basic game theory, land is a divisible good: you can cut it up, trade it, or carve out a joint economic zone where both sides win.

Then you introduce the variable "Holy Land", and the variable "Property Deed Signed by God Himself", and what you have stops being a property dispute and becomes a theological absolute. Theological absolutes, by the way they're built, can't be compromised, and every trade-off gets relabelled as apostasy, or treason against heaven.

There's experimental evidence for this. Jeremy Ginges, Scott Atran, and colleagues offered Israeli settlers and Palestinians hypothetical peace deals that traded away something sacred, and found that sweetening the deal with money made people more opposed to it (Ginges et al., 2007).

This isn't the only way to read the conflict, and Israel-Palestine has thick colonial, ethnic, and geopolitical layers I'm not engaging with here. But the theological component is hard to pretend away.

In September 1995, Yitzhak Rabin signed Oslo II, the interim agreement that was meant to lead to talks on a permanent settlement. He wasn't a naive peacenik. He was a former general who, as far as I can tell, had run the numbers and concluded that trading land for security was the best deal Israel could get, even though he told the Knesset that October that he envisaged a Palestinian "entity which is less than a state".

Five weeks later, on 4 November 1995, Yigal Amir, a law student at Bar-Ilan University, shot Rabin in the back. Amir told police he had acted on "orders of God", and he cited _din rodef_, the "law of the pursuer" in Jewish law, which obliges a bystander to stop someone who is pursuing another person to kill them, by killing the pursuer if necessary. To Amir, giving up holy land made Rabin a pursuer of Jewish lives.

On the other side, Hamas's 1988 charter declared Palestine an Islamic waqf consecrated until Judgment Day, no part of which may be given up, and called peace initiatives and international conferences "in contradiction to the principles" of the movement. (Its 2017 policy document accepted a state on the 1967 lines as "a formula of national consensus", while still refusing to recognize Israel.) Andrew Kydd and Barbara Walter have documented how extremist attacks tend to be timed to major events in peace processes, and in early 1996 a wave of Hamas suicide bombings killed dozens of Israelis in the run-up to the election that brought Benjamin Netanyahu, an opponent of Oslo, to power.

The mechanism is the same on both sides, the same software in different versions: dogma converts divisible goods into sacred absolutes, and sacred absolutes rarely leave a resolution both sides can accept.

Religion isn't the only cause here, but I think it's hard to argue that it isn't one of the important variables.

## III.

### Glitch dos: how many QALYs is a theological certainty worth?

{{< figure src="/images/blog/religion/image3.jpeg" alt="George W. Bush" caption="<em>August 2001. George W. Bush blocks federal funding for new embryonic stem cell research lines in the US.</em>" >}}

The reasoning behind that policy was a question about the moral status of a human embryo, which for many of its supporters was a question about when a soul begins. The embryos in question were blastocysts: microscopic clusters of cells, smaller than the tip of a needle, with no nervous system, no pain receptors, and no consciousness in any sense philosophy can defend.

The opportunity cost was about seven and a half years of restricted federal funding (the policy lasted until March 2009) in the country with the best biomedical research infrastructure on Earth, in a field that held out hope for Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, type 1 diabetes, and spinal cord injury. Privately funded research could continue, but federal money was limited to cell lines that already existed.

In the QALY framework (quality-adjusted life years, the standard measure for evaluating health interventions), Parkinson's alone affects almost twelve million people worldwide. By my rough reckoning, one year of delay in finding a disease-modifying treatment costs hundreds of thousands of QALYs. Multiply that by the years of delay, and then across every other disease caught in the same bottleneck.

This number is rough and full of assumptions. We don't know that full funding would have produced faster breakthroughs, and stem cell research kept going elsewhere. In fact, the biggest stem cell advance of that decade, Shinya Yamanaka's induced pluripotent stem cells in 2006, came from Japan and sidestepped embryos altogether, which cuts against my argument a little. But the direction of the argument is, to my eye, hard to ignore: a policy grounded in a theological belief about the moral status of a cluster of cells can slow down treatment for millions of human beings who already exist.

And that's one policy, in one country, over less than a decade. The same pattern shows up elsewhere: the Catholic Church's opposition to condoms in the middle of Africa's HIV epidemic (in 2009, flying to Cameroon, Pope Benedict XVI said distributing condoms "even aggravates the problems"), and abstinence-only sex education in the US.

## IV.

### Leaving childhood?

{{< figure src="/images/blog/religion/image4.jpeg" alt="Dr. D. Wade Clapp" caption="<em>Dr. D. Wade Clapp's work with British biotech company Healx dates to 2019. Photo by Liz Kaye, Indiana University. He uses AI to accelerate his research on tumour treatment.</em>" >}}

I know where the criticism will come from: "So you want everyone to be nihilists? Without religion, what's stopping people from being psychopaths?"

It's a fair question, and I'm not sure I have a perfect answer.

I do think it's a false dichotomy, though. You don't need fear of divine punishment to see that bombing a small child is evil; you can get there from a fairly simple first principle, which is that you don't want to be bombed and neither do they. Something like that reciprocity sits at the root of most ethics that can be defended philosophically, from Kant to Rawls, with no reference to anything supernatural.

For mass coordination, we already have something like a substitute. It isn't perfect, but it can be updated: constitutions, universal human rights, the scientific method, and international law are all systems that can be revised when they're shown to be wrong.

These secular systems have their own failure modes, and I should list them. Constitutions can be amended for authoritarian ends. International law often has no teeth. The scientific method has been abused for eugenics. And secular institutions can become dogmatic in their own way; watch some political ideologies operate and you'll see something almost indistinguishable from religion, complete with heretics and excommunication. So I'm not selling a secular utopia.

What I'm trying to say is more specific. Notice when you're making a decision based on data and reason, and notice when you're running software designed to keep a tribe in the desert intact two thousand years ago. The two don't have to conflict every time, but when they do, it helps to know which one is talking.

Rabin knew which one was talking, and it cost him his life. The stem cell researchers knew too, and it cost them the better part of a decade.

My own estimate is that the probability of an invisible hand arriving to fix climate change, prevent the next Holocaust, or stop a nuclear war is low. If that's right, the hands that have to move are ours. I could be wrong about the probability, but the stakes are too high to act as if someone else is on the job.

## V.

Go back to the behavioural economist from the beginning. If you had designed religion under those constraints, you'd be proud of it: it held together groups far larger than any primate brain could track, for thousands of years, with no police. You'd also know that nobody should ship software meant to run for millennia without an update mechanism, and this one shipped without one.

The shield that saved our ancestors was a good design for their world, and ours is a different one. I don't think it's a betrayal to put down a tool that no longer fits the task, though it would be a mistake to pretend we've already found a perfect replacement.
